Supreme Court (nUSA)

Appearance
From nUSA Wiki

Highest court of the United States The Supreme Court of the United States is the highest court in the federal judiciary. Its work encompasses review of lower-court cases, constitutional challenges, applications for emergency relief and the administration of the legal profession. The Court has also exercised Anytime Review, bringing disputes over government action directly within its constitutional scrutiny.[1][2]

The Court's history includes repeated struggles over the boundaries between the branches of government. In 2018 and 2019 it invalidated a compulsory administrative forum for employment complaints, stopped the creation of the State of Columbia, reviewed a President's impeachment and rejected executive control of attorney licensing. Later constitutional reorganizations altered the force of older precedent without erasing the institutional conflicts those cases recorded.[3][4][5]

Jurisdiction and procedure

Cases reach the Court through petitions for certiorari, appeals, applications, judicial complaints and requests for Anytime Review. Its docket distinguishes pending requests from accepted cases and final orders. Granting certiorari permits review; denying it leaves the Court without a merits decision on the question presented. Emergency orders may preserve or change the immediate position of the parties while leaving the final constitutional issue open.[6][7]

Anytime Review gave the Court an additional route into disputes over the actions of government. In the Columbia litigation, the Court initiated review alongside a petition brought by Kirkman. In Procursive's impeachment challenge, it held that the political-question objections associated with ordinary impeachment review did not prevent consideration in the Anytime Review context. The power made the Court a participant in constitutional confrontations that extended beyond appeals from routine civil or criminal judgments.[8][9]

Constitutional decisions, 2018–2019

Federal employment and access to court

In Benda v. United States, decided 21 October 2018, the Court struck section 401(g) of the Consolidated Appropriations and Authorizations Act of August 2018. The provision forced federal employees to submit employment complaints to the National Labor Authority, later the Office of the Federal Inspector General, before seeking judicial relief. Chief Justice Holmes's opinion rejected the body's broad, compulsory and effectively unreviewable authority. Employees could thereafter take claims such as unlawful termination directly to court.[10]

The decision combined an Article III analysis with objections grounded in basic justice and rational justification. Holmes was joined by Marshall, Bork and O'Connor; Gorsuch and Stewart dissented from the judgment. The case illustrated that describing a dispute as a public right did not end the constitutional inquiry into administrative adjudication.[11]

The State of Columbia

On 3 March 2019, In re Federal Government and Kirkman v. State of Columbia brought the proposed union of Washington and Las Vegas before the Court. Congress had omitted the consent of the municipalities' legislatures. The Court invalidated sections 101–104 of S. 127 and struck the dependent agency-transfer and court provisions in sections 106–107. The decision prevented the State of Columbia from acquiring a lawful governing structure and emphasized constitutional limits on federal attempts to reorganize local government.[12]

Procursive's impeachment

The impeachment of Procursive tested the Court's authority over Congress's most consequential sanction. After a Senate conviction on four articles, Procursive asked the Court to undo his removal. On 7 May it rejected his oath and due-process arguments, invalidated three articles, and sustained the false-arrest article. The President remained out of office even though most of the charges against him failed constitutional review.[13]

The separate opinions exposed disagreement over the Ichigo standard requiring an impeachable offense to qualify at least as a crime or misdemeanor. Brandeis advocated a narrower role for judicial review and would have abandoned that standard. The case produced a divided constitutional analysis without a divided practical outcome: Procursive was not reinstated.[14]

Control of the legal profession

In Party v. Board of Law Examiners, decided 28 July 2019, the Court struck S. 147 in its entirety. Kirkman's original challenge to executive administration of the Bar had resulted in an injunction; after he and the Board sought to dissolve it, SheldonParty continued the appeal as substituted class representative. Justice Pitney's opinion held that attorney licensing was judicial power and could not be transferred to the executive. Bork and Chase agreed with that result while preserving Congress's authority to legislate on the Bar's structure.[15]

The decision forms a central chapter in the history of the Federal Bar. It distinguished judicial control from an unrestricted right to practice law: the Court rejected the latter proposition while defending the former.[16]

Orders, denials and separate statements

Not every important controversy produced a binding merits ruling. In Psychodynamic v. Procursive, the Court denied review of Procursive's withdrawn resignation. Pitney and Chase, joined by three other justices, explained in a nonbinding statement that a resignation could be recalled before it took effect. Their reasoning illuminated the controversy without making the denial itself a constitutional judgment.[17]

In Hudson v. House of Representatives, the Court denied review of a challenge to House committee subpoenas directed at three federal judges. The subpoenas had been rescinded. Bork's concurrence, joined by Chase, criticized legislative inquiries into judicial decisions, but that separate opinion did not transform the denial into a binding prohibition on congressional subpoenas.[18]

Other 2019 dispositions resolved narrower procedural matters: Kirkman v. Bank of America concerned prejudice in a District Court dismissal, Ex parte Haven became moot, and a certified question in Board of Law Examiners v. [name withheld] was dismissed as improperly filed. Together with the major opinions, these orders show a court occupied with both constitutional disputes and the discipline of ordinary appellate procedure.[19][20][21]

Membership, administration and the Bar

The Court maintains membership and officer histories, term records, dockets, order lists, administrative directives and bound volumes. Its recorded term system has included four-month terms named after chief justices. Admission to practice and the work of clerks form part of its institutional administration as well as its adjudicatory work.[22]

In July 2022, Chief Justice RummyLeroux proposed a Federal Bar Association subordinate to the Court, with licensing, ethics requirements and a path toward clerkships and judicial appointments. The proposal linked professional standards to the approaching opening of Boulder County and retained a separate Supreme Court Bar with broader practice privileges.[23]

Resets and later constitutional disputes

The July 2023 legal reset voided existing case law and court precedent alongside other legal materials. The replacement framework imposed archival duties and created new records arrangements for NARA and Congress. Older judgments remained part of the nation's history, while their former precedential authority did not automatically carry into the new legal order.[24]

An August 2024 archives update counted six Supreme Court opinions issued during July. In May 2025, the Court became entangled in a wider dispute involving a presidential impeachment, the removal of the chief justice, sanctions against two associate justices and Justice Leroux's refusal to certify the impeachment. The President remained in office pending consideration by a three-judge District Court panel. The crisis placed the independence and continuity of the judiciary alongside the disputed legitimacy of the impeachment itself.[25]
