2019 constitutional decision on presidential impeachment

Cursive v. United States
Supreme Court of the United States
Full case name Procursive v. United States, et al.
Docket 07–05
Argued 17 April 2019
Decided 7 May 2019
Proceeding Writ of review to the United States Government
Holding Articles 2–4 invalidated; Article 1 upheld; request to vacate removal denied
Principal opinion Chief Justice Holmes; different justices joined different parts
Separate opinions Bork, Stewart, Pitney, Chase and Brandeis
Nonparticipation O'Connor
Issues Impeachment; Anytime Review; congressional oaths; due process; executive privilege

Cursive v. United States, No. 07–05, was a constitutional decision of the Supreme Court concerning the impeachment and removal of President Procursive. Decided on 7 May 2019, it invalidated three of the four articles on which the Senate had convicted the President but upheld the remaining article. The Court consequently denied his request to vacate the removal. The judgment established that a President could prevail on substantial constitutional objections to impeachment without recovering the office.[1]

The opinions divided over both the meaning of high crimes and misdemeanors and the judiciary's role in reviewing Congress. Chief Justice Holmes treated the case as reviewable through Anytime Review and applied the Ichigo precedents to the charges. The justices sustaining Article 1 nevertheless included critics of its use, while Brandeis favored a much narrower judicial role. The decision's practical outcome concealed substantial disagreement over the route to it.[2]

Background

Procursive had directed the Secret Service before joining Dralian's presidential ticket. When Dralian declined to assume another term, Procursive became President-elect and took office in March 2019. His limited political experience and difficult relations with Congress formed the setting for the ensuing dispute. Around 23 March, the House Foreign Affairs Committee began investigating allegations involving denial-of-service attacks and exposure of private information by people connected with the intelligence community.[3]

Procursive invoked executive privilege rather than testify under the committee's subpoena. Further allegations concerned his cousin's employment on his Secret Service detail and JamesMontagu's appointment in the White House. An earlier allegation of false arrest supplied a fourth strand of the controversy. On 31 March, the House unanimously adopted articles addressing false arrest, withholding information, nepotism and the asserted national-security consequences of Montagu's employment.[4]

Senate proceedings

Holmes convened the Senate, House managers exhibited the articles, and senators took an oath or affirmation of impartiality. The chamber adjourned to allow preparation. During the trial, both sides presented opening statements and examined evidence, but closing statements were omitted because of time constraints. The Senate convicted on all four articles, removed Procursive and declined to disqualify him from future office.[5]

Procursive had not sought a pre-emptive judicial stay before conviction. His challenge therefore sought to undo a removal that had already occurred. He disputed congressional oaths, the adequacy of the trial and the substantive validity of the articles. The Court heard argument on 17 April.[6]

Questions before the Court

The case presented a threshold question of justiciability: whether impeachment was solely Congress's concern or could be reviewed judicially. Beyond that, the Court considered whether the President had demonstrated invalid congressional oaths, whether the Senate had afforded constitutionally sufficient process, and whether the charged conduct met the impeachment standard established by the Ichigo cases.[7]

Article 1 posed a further question because it concerned pre-presidential conduct. The Court had to consider whether an offense unrelated to the acquisition of the presidency was categorically beyond impeachment and whether a stricter inquiry into Congress's motives was justified.[8]

Decision

Anytime Review and procedure

The Court rejected the contention that the political-question doctrine necessarily excluded review. It treated the doctrine and the restrictions associated with Nixon v. United States as matters of judicial policy and prudential concern rather than constitutional commands applicable in every setting. Those justifications did not foreclose the questions presented through Anytime Review.[9]

Procursive's oath challenge failed because he had not produced sufficient evidence that members of Congress were constitutionally unsworn. His due-process challenge also failed. Impeachment was not an ordinary criminal prosecution, and the Senate did not have to reproduce all the procedures of a criminal court. The Court additionally concluded that the process provided had been adequate, including the time allowed for preparation and the defense's opportunity to present its case.[10]

Articles 2, 3 and 4

The Court reaffirmed the Ichigo principle that a high crime or misdemeanor had to qualify at least as a crime or misdemeanor. It invalidated Articles 2, 3 and 4 because they failed to allege legitimate criminal offenses under that standard. The information-withholding article also raised concerns of executive privilege. The invalidations rejected those grounds of impeachment without requiring the Court to uphold every policy or appointment that had prompted congressional criticism.[11]

Article 1 and removal

Holmes, joined on this portion by Gorsuch, Bork and Stewart, declined to find Article 1 unconstitutional. The Court did not categorically prohibit impeachment for misconduct predating the presidency. Procursive had not advanced a more demanding standard than Ichigo or supplied sufficient evidence of constitutionally impermissible motives to justify closer scrutiny. Congress's factual conclusions were not found clearly erroneous.[12]

Holmes's refusal to invalidate the charge was accompanied by a forceful criticism of its use. He regarded removal for the earlier false arrest as a serious misuse of impeachment and warned that comparable articles might fail in future cases. The judgment thus sustained the result without offering unqualified approval of Congress's conduct. With Article 1 surviving, the Court denied reinstatement.[13]

Separate opinions

Bork and Stewart

Bork joined Holmes's opinion but emphasized that failure to establish unconstitutionality was not the same as demonstrating that Congress had acted permissibly. He questioned whether the Ichigo test sufficiently addressed the word high and suggested that ordinary criminality might not settle the constitutional inquiry into impeachment.[14]

Stewart concurred in part and in the judgment. Although he had concerns about Ichigo, he regarded the absence of a properly briefed request to overturn it as decisive. The parties' reliance on the existing standard made this case an unsuitable vehicle for abandoning it. He also found insufficient evidence for the oath and due-process objections.[15]

Pitney and Chase

Pitney and Chase disagreed with the disposition of Article 1. Chase argued that a minor false-arrest offense could not reasonably satisfy the requirement of a high crime and warned against turning impeachment into a means of making the executive dependent on legislative political will. Pitney and Douglas joined the first part of Chase's opinion. Chase separately disputed the treatment of due process, favoring a fairness requirement suited to impeachment without importing every rule of an ordinary trial.[16]

Brandeis

Brandeis concurred in the judgments concerning Articles 1 and 2 but dissented on Articles 3 and 4. He favored overruling Ichigo and limiting judicial intervention in impeachment to narrow circumstances implicating separation of powers. His position sustained Article 1 through a different understanding of the judiciary's role from that used by Holmes.[17]

Aftermath and significance

Procursive remained removed, while Bakedgoods continued the presidency to which he had succeeded. The decision became an important expression of the Court's power to review individual impeachment charges. It also demonstrated that separate articles could receive different constitutional dispositions without requiring the entire Senate judgment to fall.[18]

The case influenced the reasoning of Bork's concurrence in Hudson v. House of Representatives the following month. He invoked its discussion of limits on impeachment in criticizing subpoenas directed at judges in response to their rulings. That concurrence remained nonbinding. The broader body of case law was later affected by the 2023 nUSA legal reset, which voided existing precedent.[19][20]
