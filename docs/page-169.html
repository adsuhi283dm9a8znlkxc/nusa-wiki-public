Impeachment of Procursive

  • Page
  • Discussion
Appearance
From nUSA Wiki

2019 impeachment, removal and constitutional review of President Procursive The impeachment of Procursive was a confrontation between the presidency and Congress that ended in the President's removal from office in 2019. The House unanimously adopted four articles on 31 March, and the Senate subsequently convicted him on all four. Procursive challenged the result before the Supreme Court. On 7 May, the Court invalidated three articles but upheld the remaining charge, leaving his removal in force.[1]

The case brought two questions into direct conflict: how far Congress could go in removing a President, and how far the judiciary could go in reviewing that removal. Procursive won significant parts of his constitutional challenge without recovering the presidency. The Senate had declined to disqualify him from future office, so the judgment sustained his removal rather than a permanent prohibition on public service.[2]

A presidency under pressure

Procursive entered office after Dralian declined to assume another term. His experience lay chiefly in the Secret Service, which he had directed before becoming Dralian's running mate. Once President, he encountered congressional opposition and secured confirmation of Bakedgoods as Vice President, but his relationship with Congress soon deteriorated.[3]

Around 23 March, the House Foreign Affairs Committee opened an investigation into allegations that people connected with the administration's intelligence community had participated in denial-of-service attacks and the exposure of private information. The committee subpoenaed Procursive. He invoked executive privilege and declined to testify. The inquiry was followed by allegations that he had employed his cousin on his Secret Service detail and endangered national security by hiring JamesMontagu in the White House. Those allegations joined an older accusation of false arrest from his law-enforcement service.[4]

Articles of impeachment

Article Allegation Supreme Court disposition
1 False arrest, arising from conduct before the presidency Upheld; removal remained in force
2 Withholding information from Congress Invalidated
3 Violation of an anti-nepotism statute Invalidated
4 Jeopardizing national security by employing JamesMontagu Invalidated

The House's unanimous vote on 31 March sent all four articles to the Senate. The charges combined an alleged criminal act with objections to presidential secrecy and appointments. That distinction became central to the eventual judicial decision: the Court rejected the latter three articles because they did not allege legitimate criminal offenses under the impeachment standard it applied.[5]

The withdrawn resignation

During the confrontation, Procursive attempted to resign with effect at midnight and then withdrew the resignation before that time. A challenge to the withdrawal reached the Supreme Court in Psychodynamic v. Procursive, also styled Dynamic v. Cursive. The Court denied review on 4 April.[6]

Justices Pitney and Chase, joined by Stewart, Brandeis and Douglas, issued a statement defending the distinction between an announced resignation and one that had already taken effect. They reasoned that a resignation could be withdrawn before its effective time but could not be recalled after it had become final. Their statement accompanied a denial of review; it did not constitute a binding judgment on the merits. Procursive's departure therefore cannot be understood simply as an uncontested voluntary resignation.[7]

Senate trial and removal

Chief Justice Holmes convened the Senate to receive the articles. House managers presented them, and senators took an oath or affirmation of impartiality. The chamber then adjourned to give the prosecution and defense time to prepare. At trial, both sides made opening statements and examined evidence. Time constraints led the Senate to dispense with closing statements; attempts to shorten the defense's allotted time were not allowed.[8]

The Senate convicted Procursive on every article and removed him, while declining to impose disqualification from future office. Procursive had not obtained a pre-emptive judicial stay preserving his position during review. Bakedgoods was exercising the presidency by 6 April, when he issued an executive-branch employee-database memorandum.[9][10]

Constitutional review

Main article: Cursive v. United States (nUSA) The Supreme Court heard argument on 17 April and decided the case on 7 May. Procursive challenged the fairness of the Senate trial, the validity of congressional oaths and the substance of each article. The Court rejected the oath challenge for lack of sufficient evidence. It also rejected his due-process argument, explaining that impeachment did not require all the procedures of an ordinary criminal trial and that the Senate's process had been constitutionally adequate.[11]

The Court nevertheless subjected the charges themselves to review. Under its Ichigo precedents, an impeachable high crime or misdemeanor had to qualify at least as a crime or misdemeanor. Articles 2, 3 and 4 failed that standard; the information-withholding article also raised questions of executive privilege. Article 1 survived. The Court did not categorically exclude misconduct predating the presidency from impeachment and found no sufficient basis for closer scrutiny of Congress's motives in this instance.[12]

The justices disagreed over the proper reach of judicial intervention. Brandeis would have abandoned the Ichigo standard and confined review to narrow circumstances involving separation of powers. Other separate opinions disputed the treatment of Article 1 and the meaning of high crimes and misdemeanors. The final result combined a substantial rebuke to Congress's charging decisions with the survival of its most consequential action: removing the President.[13]

Historical significance

The impeachment demonstrated that a congressional conviction could survive even when most of its supporting articles were unconstitutional. It also made Anytime Review an important instrument in the struggle between Congress and the presidency. Those decisions belong to the constitutional order preceding the 2023 legal reset, which later voided existing case law and precedent; their historical significance should be distinguished from their continuing legal force.[14]
