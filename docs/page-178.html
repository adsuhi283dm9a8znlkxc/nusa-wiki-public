2019 decision invalidating executive control of attorney licensing

Party v. Board of Law Examiners
Supreme Court of the United States
Full case name SheldonParty v. Board of Law Examiners, et al.
Docket 07–18
Decided 28 July 2019
Prior proceedings District Court injunction; Court of Appeals vacated injunction
Holding Court of Appeals reversed; S. 147 struck down in its entirety
Opinion Pitney, joined in full by Holmes, Gorsuch, O’Connor, Stewart and Douglas
Concurrence in part Bork, joined by Chase
Nonparticipation Brandeis
Issue Separation of powers and attorney licensing

Party v. Board of Law Examiners, No. 07–18, was a Supreme Court decision of 28 July 2019 invalidating S. 147, legislation placing administration of the Bar under executive control. The Court held that licensing attorneys was an exercise of judicial power and could not be transferred to the executive without violating the separation of powers. It also reversed the Court of Appeals for vacating an injunction without affirmative legal arguments supporting that action.[1]

The case emerged from a class action originally led by J. A. Kirkman. SheldonParty became class representative after Kirkman and the Board sought to dissolve the injunction against the licensing arrangement. His intervention prevented their agreement from ending the challenge to the allocation of power between the branches.[2]

Background

The Consolidated Appropriations and Authorizations Act of August 2018 had placed a Bar system within the executive branch. Ultiman v. United States challenged that arrangement, but statutory changes made the case moot before the Court resolved its separation-of-powers concerns. The constitutional issue returned when the Reviving the Bar Act, old S. 147, again placed administration under executive control.[3][4]

President Bakedgoods signed S. 147 on 27 April 2019. Kirkman challenged the law on behalf of Supreme Court Bar members. The District Court entered judgment against the arrangement and issued an injunction. Kirkman then helped develop a successor, the Private Representation Revitalization Act, new S. 147, addressing concerns specific to existing practitioners while retaining the disputed allocation of administrative authority.[5]

Lower-court proceedings and substitution

Kirkman and the Board sought dissolution of the injunction after the successor measure became law. The Court of Appeals vacated it without a presentation affirmatively challenging it on the merits. SheldonParty, a member of the plaintiff class, sought substitution as representative so that he could continue the appeal. The Supreme Court granted substitution.[6]

A 29 May stay application in In re Kirkman had been denied without finally settling the merits. On 17 June, the Supreme Court temporarily enjoined both versions of S. 147 pending its disposition of SheldonParty's appeal. Those orders formed intermediate stages rather than substitutes for the final constitutional ruling.[7][8]

Issues and arguments

SheldonParty argued that the Court of Appeals had erred by vacating the injunction without legal grounds and that both versions of S. 147 were unconstitutional. The appeal required the Court to distinguish an agreement between litigants from an adequate legal basis for reversing judicial relief. It also presented the unresolved question from the earlier Bar controversy: could the executive regulate admission of officers practicing before the courts?[9]

The litigation also raised a due-process theory associated with the burdens placed on attorneys. The Court distinguished injury sufficient to establish standing from a constitutional right to practice law. Establishing that regulation affected clients and income did not by itself establish that the Constitution guaranteed professional admission.[10]

Opinion of the Court

Justice Pitney held that the Court of Appeals had exceeded its authority by vacating the injunction without legal arguments supporting the action. The absence of a party wishing to maintain the injunction did not answer whether the underlying constitutional defect remained. The Court reversed that appellate disposition.[11]

On the merits, the Court treated admission and exclusion of attorneys as judicial functions. Attorneys were officers of the courts, and control over their admission and discipline was necessary to the administration of justice. Executive selection and regulation of those officers would interfere with judicial power vested in the Supreme Court and inferior courts. The opinion struck S. 147 down in its entirety.[12]

The Court rejected the separate claim of a constitutional right to practice law. Its holding protected judicial administration of the profession rather than removing the judiciary's ability to impose admission standards. Holmes, Gorsuch, O'Connor, Stewart and Douglas joined Pitney's opinion in full; Bork and Chase joined in part, and Brandeis did not participate.[13]

Bork's concurrence

Bork, joined by Chase, agreed that executive administration violated judicial power but cautioned against a broader exclusion of Congress. In his view, Congress could regulate the Bar's form and structure so long as administration stayed with the judiciary. His partial concurrence confined the structural objection to the transfer of administration rather than all legislative involvement in professional regulation.[14]

Subsequent developments

The case became a central part of the Federal Bar's institutional history. In July 2022, Chief Justice RummyLeroux announced a planned association subordinate to the Supreme Court, combining licensing, ethics and professional development with preparations for paid legal services in Boulder County. The memorandum was advance notice of a forthcoming directive; its significance was the intended placement of regulation within the judiciary.[15]

The 2023 legal reset later voided existing case law and precedent. The 2019 judgment remains part of the historical explanation for the struggle over Bar control, while its pre-reset legal force should not automatically be treated as continuing unchanged.[16]
