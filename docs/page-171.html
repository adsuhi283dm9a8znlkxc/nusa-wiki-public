2018 decision on federal employment complaints and judicial power

Benda v. United States
Supreme Court of the United States
Full case name Benda587 v. United States
Docket 05–75
Decided 21 October 2018
Prior proceedings Certified question from District Court case 3:18–1658
Holding Section 401(g) unconstitutional on its face
Opinion Holmes, joined by Marshall, Bork and O’Connor
Dissent from judgment Gorsuch and Stewart
Nonparticipation Kavanaugh, Kennedy and Jackson
Law applied Article III; section 401(g) of the August 2018 appropriations legislation

Benda v. United States was a Supreme Court decision of 21 October 2018 that invalidated a mandatory administrative barrier to litigation by federal employees. Section 401(g) of the Consolidated Appropriations and Authorizations Act of August 2018 required complaints within the National Labor Authority's jurisdiction to be heard by that body before a court could consider them. The Court held the provision unconstitutional on its face, allowing employees to bring unlawful-employment complaints directly to court.[1][2]

The dispute concerned more than the order in which a complaint was filed. It tested whether an executive institution could exercise broad and effectively unreviewable authority over disputes involving the government as employer. The Court's answer linked access to justice with the constitutional division between administrative government and independent adjudication.[3]

Background

Benda587 filed suit in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia, challenging the requirement under Article III. The District Court certified the constitutional question to the Supreme Court in case 3:18–1658. The administrative body named in the legislation, the National Labor Authority, had become known as the Office of the Federal Inspector General by the time of the decision.[4]

Congress's requirement placed a compulsory first hearing between an employee and a court. A worker alleging unlawful dismissal or another unlawful employment action could not simply choose a judicial forum. The statute did not make that route depend on the worker's consent, and the breadth of the authority's jurisdiction made the restriction important across federal employment.[5]

Article III analysis

Chief Justice Holmes approached the question through the distinction between public and private rights. Congress had greater latitude to assign public-rights disputes to bodies outside Article III, and the Court accepted that most of the matters covered by section 401(g) fell within that category. It nevertheless refused to treat the label as decisive. The nature and importance of the rights, the tribunal's power, consent and Congress's reasons for choosing the administrative forum all remained relevant.[6]

The Court found several defects working together. The employment rights involved were important to accountability, transparency and electoral freedom. The authority possessed unusually broad jurisdiction, its decisions were effectively unreviewable, and employees were compelled to submit to it. Congress had supplied no sufficient justification for imposing that arrangement. The Court therefore concluded that section 401(g) exceeded constitutional limits on nonjudicial adjudication.[7]

Justice and rational justification

The opinion also identified independent grounds for invalidation. Legislation governing judicial procedure could not contradict basic principles of justice or lack any rational justification. Applying those principles, the Court found the compulsory barrier unjustifiable under the circumstances. Its reasoning thus combined a structural Article III analysis with objections to the fairness and purpose of the procedure itself.[8]

Holmes's opinion was joined by Justices Marshall, Bork and O'Connor. Gorsuch and Stewart dissented from the judgment; Kavanaugh, Kennedy and Jackson did not participate. The decision invalidated the challenged statutory requirement, rather than adjudicating every federal employment grievance or guaranteeing that an employee would prevail on the merits.[9]

Consequences

The immediate consequence was to remove the compulsory administrative first hearing for claims such as unlawful termination. Federal employees gained the option of seeking judicial relief directly. In the institutional history of the period, Benda sits alongside the later Bar litigation: both controversies concerned attempts to place functions closely connected with the courts under executive authority, although they involved different laws and different legal questions.[10][11]

The ruling belongs to the body of pre-2023 case law later affected by the legal reset. Its place in the history of employment rights and judicial independence remains distinct from the question of whether its precedent continued to govern after that reset.[12]
