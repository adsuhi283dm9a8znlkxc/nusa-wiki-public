2019 decision invalidating the State of Columbia's governing framework

In re Federal Government
Supreme Court of the United States
Consolidated case Kirkman v. State of Columbia, et al.
Dockets 07–01 and 07–02
Decided 3 March 2019
Proceeding Anytime Review, including Court-initiated review
Opinion Per curiam
Holding Sections 101–104 of S. 127 unconstitutional; sections 106–107 inseverable and invalid
Law applied Constitution, Article IV, Section 3
Subject Municipal consent and severability

In re Federal Government, heard with Kirkman v. State of Columbia, was a Supreme Court decision of 3 March 2019 invalidating the principal provisions of S. 127, which sought to combine Washington, D.C., and Las Vegas into the State of Columbia. The Court held that the merger lacked the constitutionally required consent of both municipal legislatures. It also struck dependent agency-transfer and court provisions because they could not operate independently of the invalid merger.[1]

The per curiam ruling combined a municipal-autonomy holding with a substantial severability analysis. Its remedy preserved some ancillary provisions while preventing the proposed state from acquiring its governor, transferred agencies and judicial system. The Court also used the case to warn that officials remained bound by constitutional limits even when they anticipated judicial restraint.[2]

Background

Congress passed S. 127 as a plan for reorganizing municipal government. The Act asserted a union of Las Vegas and Washington, named the new jurisdiction Columbia, and supplied a provisional governor exercising interim authority. It also provided for agency transfers and a court system. Columbia's creation was publicly announced on 2 March.[3][4][5]

Article IV, Section 3 prohibited the joining of existing municipalities without the consent of the affected legislatures. The statute relied in part on H.R. 179's supposed demunicipalization of Las Vegas, but the Supreme Court noted that the measure itself referred to Las Vegas as a municipality. The Columbia dispute reached the Court through both Court-initiated review and Kirkman's petition.[6]

Constitutional issue

The central question was whether Washington and Las Vegas had consented to the merger. The Court found no record of a vote by either municipality approving the union. It required consent that was clear, public and unambiguous. The possibility that municipal authorities had received notice could not substitute for the affirmative legislative approval required by the Constitution.[7]

The Court did not decide H.R. 179's legality. Even assuming that measure valid, its language did not remove the constitutional problem presented by joining two municipalities without consent. The federal legislature could not circumvent the consent requirement through an unsupported description of Las Vegas's status.[8]

Decision

Invalid merger provisions

The Court struck sections 101 through 104. Section 101 asserted the merger; section 102 named the jurisdiction and assigned its march and anthem; section 103 declared an intention to establish it swiftly; and section 104 created the provisional government. Each depended on the unlawful union, and the provisional government attempted to transfer the existing municipalities' sovereign powers to a governor they had not authorized.[9]

Severability

S. 127 contained a severability clause, expressing Congress's preference that valid portions survive. The Court nevertheless declined to rewrite interdependent provisions to make them workable. A severability clause could guide the remedy but could not require the judiciary to perform legislative reconstruction.[10]

Section 106 required transfers of agencies to Columbia's provisional governor. With the governor's office invalid, the provision had no meaningful use. Section 107 established courts whose appointment system and duties depended on Columbia's institutions and laws. Removing those dependencies would strip the offices of their purpose and redesign the law. Both sections were therefore inseverable and were struck in full.[11]

The effective-date clause, severability clause and section 105 disclaimer concerning development were preserved. They either had no independent operative effect or confirmed the existing position without depending on the unlawful merger. The Court thus invalidated Columbia's governing machinery rather than the whole statute indiscriminately.[12]

Aftermath and significance

The ruling ended the statutory framework for Columbia a day after the public creation announcement. pauljkl separately announced its dissolution through founder authority. The judicial decision established the constitutional defect in the merger, while the separate dissolution represented another exercise of authority against the project.[13][14]

The Court's concluding warning emphasized that constitutional duties did not depend on whether a court was expected to intervene. Officials could not expand their authority through manipulation of statutory forms. In institutional terms, the case demonstrated both the protection of municipal consent and the reach of Anytime Review in disputes over the structure of government.[15]
