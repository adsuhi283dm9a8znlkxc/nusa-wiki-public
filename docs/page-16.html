Federal Election Commission (nUSA)

  • Page
  • Discussion
Appearance
From nUSA Wiki

The Federal Election Commission (nUSA) (FEC) was an election-administration body in the Roblox-based nUSA roleplay community. Its records describe work on federal and congressional elections, including setting or applying rules, managing registration and voting, reviewing ballots, and certifying results. The Commission used committees and election officers, with the National Elections Administrator appearing as a senior officer in its publications.[1]

Organization and remit

The FEC's January 2020 regulations say the Commission prepared a public compilation of election legislation and internal rules that applied to elections it administered. The document lists congressional and presidential committees, statutory officers, investigators, and the Office of the National Elections Administrator. It also describes candidate eligibility review and ballot administration under the rules it compiled.[2]

The Commission's procedures assigned it authority to review candidate eligibility, manage election incidents, and invalidate ballots for violations of its regulations. The publication assigned the National Elections Administrator authority to remove election officers for specified violations, including unauthorized release of election material. These are provisions in the 2020 publication, rather than evidence that every listed power was exercised in every election.[3]

Election administration

Archived announcements show the Commission announcing candidate ballots, opening voting, handling complaints, and delivering results to congressional officers. In May 2019, for example, it announced that a filtering delay affected the release of results, then reported certification and delivery of election results to the Speaker of the House. During the Summer 2019 presidential election, its National Elections Administrator delivered results to Congress and named a President-elect and vice-President-elect.[4]

The Commission's name appears in records as both “Federal Election Commission” and “Federal Elections Commission.” Separate election committees also appear in the records: the Fall 2019 plan calls itself the Federal Elections Presidential Committee while identifying itself as an official document of, and approved by, the Federal Election Commission. The records therefore support distinguishing the committee from the Commission rather than treating “FEPC” and “FEC” as interchangeable names.[5]

Later records

In September 2023, Group Management thanked goleros for leading both the FEC and the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), documenting overlapping leadership at that time. An October 2023 appointment notice named TacticalRummy as National Elections Administrator while also assigning responsibility for running NARA. In June 2024, Group Management said the Government GM liaison posts would be merged “with NARA remaining separate”; that notice described NARA's relation to the management structure and did not announce a merger of NARA and the FEC.[6]

A June 2024 announcement said Group Management and the FEC had collaborated on “MultiBallot,” a proposed redesign of congressional seat elections and political parties. The separately titled MultiBallot directive of 2024 describes the new system; the announcement and design document establish what was announced, while the reviewed records do not by themselves show how consistently the system was used afterward.[7]

Rules and public oversight

The Commission's public rules changed over time. In May 2024, it moved the usual start of campaigning to 14 days before registration opened, with presidential campaign periods announced separately. Its June 2024 notices also published an eligibility board listing Roblox user IDs the FEC considered valid or ineligible and allowed members to challenge a listing through a ticket. A separate evidence notice rejected screenshots and required unedited GIFs or videos showing a Discord refresh or reopen cycle. These notices document the Commission's practice in 2024; they should not be treated as a complete or current code.[8]

In December 2025, the FEC released a 20-page Federal Election Code that it described as a public compilation of rules enforced by the Commission. Its preamble says the code's rules were internally enacted, applied to FEC-managed elections, and could be varied or updated by the Commission. The code covers party primaries, candidate and voter eligibility, screening, evidence, appeals, election records, campaign conduct, polling safety, government and military restrictions, and political activity by government employees. Its signature schedule varied by office and expressly allowed the National Elections Administrator to change or waive thresholds. Later announcements document amendments, so the December 2025 edition is a dated snapshot rather than a complete statement of later practice.[9]

The FEC's procedures were also tested by disputes and errors. In June 2024, a public notice said Senior Commissioner SolarGeneral had been temporarily suspended while the Commission reviewed internal procedures and staff; the archive contains no conclusion to that review. During the March 2026 presidential election, a management statement described an internal review of the filtering process and a blind re-filtering of ballots after it said an official had accessed live vote data during filtering. The resulting recount changed the announced result and prompted a stated safeguard against immediate result display. The event is described separately at Spring 2026 presidential election recount (nUSA), with the claims attributed to the management statement.[10]

Election systems and certification

The Commission worked with management and developers on MultiBallot, which was tested in June 2024 and first used for a House Class II election in July. Announcements documented a further backend test, the first operational election, and an FEC-certified result, providing evidence of implementation beyond the design document.[11]

In September 2026, the National Elections Administrator said certification of winners in FEC-managed federal and municipal elections belonged solely to the Commission and directed judges to administer oaths to House Class II winners who had not been sworn in by the House. This notice records the NEA's stated view during a seating dispute; the available archive does not document the House's response or a final resolution.[12]
