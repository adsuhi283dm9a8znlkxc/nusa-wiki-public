State of Columbia (nUSA)

  • Page
  • Discussion
Appearance
From nUSA Wiki

Short-lived 2019 attempt to unite Washington and Las Vegas The State of Columbia was a short-lived attempt to unite Washington, D.C., and Las Vegas under a single municipal government in March 2019. Congress's S. 127 sought to merge the cities, install a provisional government and establish a new judicial system. On 3 March, the Supreme Court invalidated the merger and its principal supporting provisions because neither city's legislature had consented to the union.[1]

The project offered a sweeping replacement for the existing municipal order. Within a day of its public creation announcement, however, its legal foundation had collapsed. Columbia's failure became a constitutional warning that federal authority over local government did not include an unrestricted power to combine municipalities against their will.[2][3]

The municipal reorganization

S. 127, titled To restore municipal government, envisioned a merger of Las Vegas and the District of Columbia into a single jurisdiction bearing the name State of Columbia. Its framework included a provisional governor appointed by the President with Senate consent. The governor would exercise interim executive and legislative authority while negotiating a constitution for submission to the two city councils. The councils were to retain their development responsibilities.[4]

The measure supplied more than a new name. It assigned a march and anthem, contemplated the transfer of certain federal agencies and provided for a State Court of Columbia. Its judicial provisions also described a State Crime Panel within the federal District Court and addressed regulation of the legal profession. These provisions sought to create the apparatus of government around the proposed merger.[5]

At 5:05 p.m. on 2 March, azahid1 publicly announced Columbia's creation. The Supreme Court took up the recently passed legislation both on its own initiative in In re Federal Government and through Kirkman's challenge to the State of Columbia. Its intervention arrived before the proposed constitutional and administrative settlement could become an enduring municipal order.[6][7]

The missing consent

Main article: In re Federal Government (nUSA) Article IV, Section 3 prohibited a municipality formed by joining existing municipalities without the consent of their legislatures. The Court found no record of either city voting to approve the merger. It required clear, public and unambiguous consent from both municipalities; prior notice, even if supplied, could not substitute for that consent.[8]

The legislation tried to justify the merger by referring to the demunicipalization of Las Vegas under H.R. 179. The Court rejected that route around the consent requirement. H.R. 179 itself referred to Las Vegas as a municipality, and its legality was not being decided in the Columbia case. For purposes of the dispute, both Las Vegas and Washington retained the municipal status that made their consent indispensable.[9]

The Court struck sections 101 through 104: the merger, naming provisions, declaration of congressional intent and provisional government. Without a valid union, Congress could not transfer the cities' sovereign powers to Columbia's governor. The new government's existence depended on precisely the constitutional step that had been omitted.[10]

Why the remaining institutions fell

S. 127 included a severability clause, but that did not preserve every provision once the merger had failed. Section 106 required agency transfers to Columbia's provisional governor. With no lawful governor to receive them, the transfers had no meaningful operation. Section 107 created courts whose appointments and duties depended on Columbia's government and laws. Saving them would have required the Court to rewrite the legislation.[11]

The Court therefore invalidated sections 106 and 107 as inseverable from the unconstitutional core. It preserved the effective-date provision, severability clause and disclaimer concerning development authority because those provisions could stand independently. The ruling destroyed Columbia's governing framework without declaring every word of S. 127 invalid.[12]

Dissolution and legacy

On 3 March, pauljkl also announced Columbia's dissolution through founder authority. The judicial ruling supplied a separate constitutional basis for the end of the merger. Later notices described the state as legally null and void, and the proposed governor, agency transfers and court system could no longer operate under the invalidated sections.[13][14][15]

The Court closed with a warning that public officials remained bound by the Constitution even when they expected the judiciary to refrain from intervention. Columbia thus became an example of the limits of federal reorganization and of the Court's willingness to use Anytime Review to enforce those limits.[16]
